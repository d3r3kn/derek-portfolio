Satellite Physics

What 16,872 tracked satellites show about how orbits work, why height sets the lag, why the hot upper air is no threat to them, and what all that means for putting data centers in orbit.

The Idea

Every working satellite is tracked, and the tracking data is public. Each record holds one key number: how many times a day the satellite circles Earth. Gravity turns that number into an altitude and a speed. The speed of light turns the altitude into the shortest possible delay for any signal that uses it.

The catalog holds active satellites. of them are Starlink, orbiting about up, where a signal's round trip can't take less than . The satellites in the geostationary belt sit at , so the same round trip can't take less than . That one difference explains why the new internet constellations fly low and need thousands of satellites.

Three things come out of the data plainly. Low orbit is the only place a satellite internet can have low lag, which is why the new constellations fly under 600 km and need thousands of satellites to do it. Every one of the satellites sits exactly where gravity says it should; not one breaks the physics. And the hot upper air is no danger to any of them: at 400 km it delivers of what sunlight does.

The charts below show each of those with the real satellites. A section further down takes up the heat doubt directly, because it is a fair question and the data answers it. The last section turns the same numbers on the newest idea in orbit: data centers.

Does the Hot Upper Air Cook Satellites?

No. The data is clear on this. The doubt is still a fair one, because the temperature is real. Above about 100 km the air is called the thermosphere because its temperature climbs with height, to at 400 km when the sun is active. The space station flies through it. So does every Starlink satellite. They don't melt, and the reason is not a technicality.

Temperature and heat are different things. Temperature says how fast the molecules move. Heat transfer depends on how many of them hit you. At 400 km the air is about times thinner than at sea level. Each molecule arrives fast, but there are so few that the energy they bring adds up to almost nothing. It is the same reason a sparkler's sparks, hotter than 1,000 °C, land on your hand without burning: each one carries almost no energy.

The energy budget on one square meter

The real problem is the opposite one. In sunlight a satellite's challenge is getting rid of heat, because there is no air to carry it away. A vacuum insulates like a thermos. The only way out is to radiate. A flat panel facing the sun with nothing else around it settles near ; the same panel in Earth's shadow, warmed only by Earth's own glow, settles near . Every satellite crosses between those two every orbit, roughly every in low orbit.

So satellites are built for radiation, not for air. Foil blankets reflect sunlight, radiator panels dump waste heat toward dark sky, and small electric heaters cover the shadow half of the orbit. None of that would be needed if the air mattered, and none of it would work if the air were thick. The thin, hot thermosphere is not the threat. The sun is.

Where the air does win. Drop to 100 km and the same calculation gives about on the front face, roughly times direct sunlight. That is re-entry, and it is why every orbit in the catalog stays well above it.

So the doubt gets a straight answer. Satellites fly through air hotter than 1,000 °C, and it does not matter, because there is almost no air. That is not a guess about satellites. It is the same atmosphere model the people who fly them use to plan their orbits, and the same sunlight figure NASA uses to balance Earth's energy budget.

What About Data Centers in Space?

The idea is real and funded. Google announced Project Suncatcher in November 2025: solar-powered satellites carrying its TPU chips, linked by laser, with two prototypes planned with Planet by early 20278. A first test satellite with four TPUs went up in October 202610. A startup called Starcloud flew a 60 kg satellite carrying an NVIDIA H100, a data-center chip, in November 20259. The pitch is the same each time: endless sun for power, and the vacuum of space as a heat sink.

Everything on this page bears on that pitch, and the numbers cut both ways. The sun part is right. The heat-sink part is half right, and the half that is wrong is the expensive half.

The budget for one megawatt of computing in low orbit

What a 1 MW facility at must carry, worked from the same constants as the rest of the page. Every watt of electricity the chips use becomes heat, and the only way to get rid of heat in a vacuum is to radiate it. Pick how hot the radiators run.

Assumptions: radiator surfaces with 90% emissivity, radiating from both faces with one face toward Earth; solar cells at 30% efficiency; no losses in batteries or power conversion, so the real areas are larger. For scale, an American football field is about 5,350 m².

Space is not a cold sink. It is an empty one. On the ground, air and water carry heat away by touch. In orbit nothing touches the radiator, so it sheds only what it can radiate, and at that is about net. A megawatt of chips needs about of radiator at that temperature. Run the chips hotter and the radiators shrink; run them cooler for longer life and the radiators grow. That is the same radiation physics that keeps the thermosphere from mattering, now working against the engineer instead of for them.

The sun is the real upside. Full sunlight is every second of the orbit in a dawn-to-dusk orbit, the orbit Google's prototype flies, with no night and no weather. At 30% cell efficiency that is of panel per megawatt. In an ordinary low orbit the worst case is of every orbit in Earth's shadow, so a facility needs about of panel plus batteries to average the same megawatt.

Lag is not the obstacle. At the round-trip floor is , the same as any other low-orbit satellite. A data center in orbit answers about as fast as one in the next state.

What this page can't settle. Launch cost: Google's own analysis puts the break-even near $200 per kilogram to orbit, a price it expects by the mid-2030s, and even then compares only to a ground data center's energy bill, not its whole cost8. Radiation: Google reports its chips survived a test dose well past a five-year mission, but that is one chip and one test8. Repair: there is no technician in orbit, and the researchers quoted on the prototype put the cost of that at ten to a hundred times what it is on the ground10. None of those is physics. All of them are money.

So the verdict is specific. The physics allows data centers in space and prices them: roughly a thousand square meters of radiator and two and a half thousand of solar panel for every megawatt, before a single kilogram is launched. The hot upper air is not the obstacle, and neither is lag. Area, launch and repair are.

How the Data Was Prepared

A Python script reads the untouched catalog, works out each orbit from its revolutions per day with the standard gravity formula, and runs the atmosphere model. Each step:

How altitude comes out of one number. A satellite that circles Earth 15.1 times a day takes 95.4 minutes per lap. For any orbit, the lap time fixes the orbit's size (Kepler's third law), so the size, and the altitude above Earth's surface, follow directly. Speed follows from the size too. Nothing is measured by hand; it is the same arithmetic every tracking program uses.

How the numbers were checked

  • The physics lands on known values. The satellites with a day-long period come out at a median altitude of , against the textbook 35,786 km. The space station comes out at , matching NASA's "about 250 miles."
  • The atmosphere model lands on known values. Its sea-level density is within 6% of the standard 1.225 kg/m³, and its upper-air temperatures fall inside the range the thermosphere is known for.
  • The catalog is whole. The script confirms the raw file's fingerprint, that every satellite appears once with a real orbit, that the orbit bands add back up to the total, and that no element set is stale.

All checks run every time the numbers are rebuilt, and the build fails if any don't pass. The script and summary data are on GitHub, with the steps to download the same catalog and confirm it matches.

What the Data Can't Tell Us

  • It covers working satellites only. Dead satellites, rocket stages and debris are tracked too, but they are in other catalog groups and are left out here.
  • Altitude is an average. Most orbits are nearly circular, but a few swing between a low point and a high point thousands of kilometers apart. Those are shown at the average of the two and labeled elliptical.
  • Signal delay here is a floor, not a measurement. It is the time light needs to go up and down twice. Real connections add routing, processing and the trip through the ground network, so measured delays are higher. The ranking between altitudes still holds.
  • The atmosphere model is a model. It gives typical values for one place and time (the equator at local noon, quiet geomagnetic conditions). The real upper air changes with latitude, time of day and solar storms. The three solar levels bracket most of that range.
  • The air-heating figures are upper bounds. They assume every molecule gives up all its energy to a cold surface. A real satellite absorbs less, so the gap with sunlight is wider, not narrower.
  • Constellations are grouped by name. A satellite is counted as Starlink if its catalog name begins with Starlink, and so on. The geostationary belt is grouped by orbit instead, since its satellites carry many names.
  • Nothing here says how any one satellite is built. Thermal design, radiator size and battery life differ from one spacecraft to the next. The page shows the environment they all share.

Glossary

Element set
The handful of numbers that describe a satellite's current orbit, published for every tracked object and refreshed as it is re-observed.
Revolutions per day
How many laps of Earth a satellite completes in 24 hours. In the catalog it is called mean motion. About 15.5 for the lowest orbits, exactly 1 for the geostationary belt.
Period
The time for one lap. 24 hours divided by revolutions per day.
Low Earth orbit
Anything below 2,000 km. Nearly every satellite is here, most of them below 600 km.
Geostationary
An orbit at 35,786 km above the equator, where one lap takes exactly one day, so the satellite stays over the same spot. Used by TV, weather and older internet satellites.
Thermosphere
The layer of the atmosphere from about 90 km up to several hundred kilometers, where the thin air is heated to hundreds or thousands of degrees by the sun's ultraviolet light.
Solar activity
How active the sun is, measured here by its radio output (the F10.7 index). It swells the upper atmosphere: the same altitude holds far more air at an active sun than at a quiet one.
W/m²
Watts per square meter: how much energy lands on one square meter each second. Direct sunlight in space is about 1,360.
