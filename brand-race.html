How 14 automakers' new vehicles changed from 1980 to 2025: speed, power, and fuel economy.

The Race

Every year the EPA publishes the average horsepower, 0–60 time, and real-world fuel economy of the new vehicles each automaker sells in the U.S. Put side by side, those numbers turn into a 45-year race.

How the Data Was Prepared

The EPA export is small enough to work with directly. A Python script reads the untouched file, keeps what the charts need, and ranks the brands. Each step and its effect on the row count:

Why rows were dropped in the last step. EPA keeps a row for every automaker and year even before a brand sold anything in the U.S., or when it sold no vehicles of a type. Those rows are empty, so they were skipped rather than counted as zero. That is why Hyundai first appears in 1990, Kia in 2000, and Tesla in 2020, and why some brands have no trucks in early snapshots.

How the numbers were checked

  • Brand totals match EPA's own math. Each automaker's all-vehicle figure equals the production-weighted blend of its car and truck figures, to within 0.11%. That confirms the rows are being read the way EPA built them.
  • The rankings are clean. In every view and year, each brand appears once, ranks run from 1 with no gaps, and there are no ties.
  • The source file is unchanged. The script confirms the raw file's fingerprint before it runs, and the row counts at every step add up.

All checks run every time the numbers are rebuilt, and the build fails if any don't pass. The script and summary data are on GitHub, along with the steps to download the same file from EPA and confirm it matches.

What the Data Can't Tell Us

  • It describes what each brand sold, not how good its cars are. A brand that sells mostly pickups averages slower and thirstier, even if its sports car is the quickest on the road. The Cars and Trucks views help compare like with like.
  • Automakers are counted as parent companies. GM includes Chevrolet, Cadillac, Buick, and GMC; Stellantis includes Jeep, Ram, Dodge, and Chrysler; Hyundai and Kia are listed separately.
  • "Truck" is a regulatory term. EPA counts most SUVs and crossovers as trucks, so the Trucks view is mostly SUVs, not just pickups.
  • Electric vehicles are rated in energy-equivalent terms. EPA counts EVs in its fuel-economy averages using the distance they travel on the energy in a gallon of gas. Tesla sells only EVs, so its figure is far above the rest. Brands selling a mix of EVs and gas vehicles get a smaller boost.
  • 0–60 times are estimates. EPA calculates them from each vehicle's power, weight, and other specifications, not from track tests.
  • 2025 is preliminary. EPA's 2025 data come from automakers' projected production before the vehicles went on sale. 1975 to 2024 are final.
  • Not every brand is here. The 14 automakers make up most of the market, but smaller brands, like Volvo, Porsche, or Mitsubishi, appear only in the industry average.

Glossary

Model year
The year an automaker assigns to a vehicle, which usually goes on sale in the fall of the year before.
Production-weighted
An average where each model counts in proportion to how many were built. A model built 300,000 times counts 100 times as much as one built 3,000 times.
Real-world MPG
EPA's estimate of fuel economy in everyday driving, lower than the older lab-test figures that regulations use.
0–60 time
Seconds to accelerate from a stop to 60 mph. Lower is quicker.
Car vs. truck
EPA's regulatory classes. Trucks include pickups, vans, and most SUVs and crossovers.
