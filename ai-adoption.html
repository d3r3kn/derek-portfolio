Why the numbers differ

Five estimates from late 2025, all describing the same economy. A Federal Reserve note says the biggest cause is likely who is sampled and what is being counted: 57% of U.S. firms have fewer than five employees, so a share of firms looks very different from a share of workers.12

These measures should not be joined into one trend line. Sources: Federal Reserve note,12 Census working paper,11 Gallup.3

How the Data Was Prepared

A Python script reads the two downloaded files without editing them, combines them with figures typed from publishers' pages, and writes the summary file these charts use.

StepWhat happened
Gallup file10 industries, 7 surveys, 3 use levels: 210 values, all kept
Census fileKept the "yes" answers on AI use for one collection period. Of 19 sectors, 17 have published values. The group for companies spanning several sectors was left out.
Published figuresGallup's all-employee numbers and the Pew, Real-Time Population Survey, and Federal Reserve figures were typed from the publishers' pages, with each link saved beside the numbers

How the numbers were checked

  • Gallup's file matches Gallup's own reports. Spot values agree with its survey tables and its indicator page.
  • Census's file matches Census's own article. Information at 39.7% and finance at 33.9% agree with its May 2026 story.
  • The values make sense together. In every industry and survey, daily use is never above frequent use, and frequent use is never above total use.
  • The source files are unchanged. The script confirms each file's fingerprint before it runs.

All checks run every time the numbers are rebuilt, and the build fails if any don't pass. The script and summary data are on GitHub, along with the steps to download the source files.

What the Data Can't Tell Us

  • These are shares, not head counts. No source here counts how many people use AI. Company user figures are left out because they could not be confirmed with the companies, and they measure accounts, not people.
  • People report their own use. Each person may picture different tools when asked about AI, and how people understand the term may have changed since 2023.
  • Small changes within an industry may be noise. Gallup publishes a margin of error for all employees, not for each industry. Industry groups are much smaller. In its survey tables through Q4 2025, the smallest had 353 people. Finance moving from 74% to 71% in Q2 2026 is not clear evidence of a drop.
  • Ten industries aren't the whole economy. Gallup's industry list is not the same as the Census list, so the two can't be matched exactly.
  • The story starts in 2023. No U.S. survey found for this page tracks people's AI use by industry before then.
  • "Total" use is a low bar. Someone who uses AI a few times a year counts the same as someone who uses it hourly. The frequency chart shows the difference.
  • Survey questions changed. Pew changed its wording in 2026. Census moved from AI use "in producing goods or services" to use "in any of its business functions" in November 2025, so its older rates aren't comparable.12 Census data is also labeled experimental.
  • Early worker survey results were revised. The Real-Time Population Survey reweighted its 2024 and early 2025 surveys after a test of question order showed they undercounted use.8
  • Census's newest files use a different name for AI. After a September 2026 executive order,13 its file now calls AI "Super Intelligence or SI (formerly referred to as Artificial Intelligence or AI)." This page says AI. The snapshot shown was collected before that order.

Glossary

Generative AI
AI that produces text, images, or other content, such as ChatGPT.
Total use
Gallup's term for using AI at work at least a few times a year.
Frequent use
Gallup's term for using AI at work at least a few times a week.
Margin of error
How far a survey result could differ from the true figure because only a sample was asked.
Employment-weighted
Counting each firm by how many people it employs, so large firms matter more.
Standard error
A measure of how much a survey estimate could vary by chance. Smaller is more precise.
